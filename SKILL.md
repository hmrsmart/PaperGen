# Humanize Writing

A checklist of the most common tells that mark text as AI-generated, compiled from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" guide and several independent write-ups on the topic. Apply this whenever drafting or revising prose so the output reads like it was written by a person who cares about the specific thing they're writing, not like a generic answer.

This is not a ban on every item below — human writers use em dashes, threes, and transitions too. The problem is *density*: when several of these stack up in the same piece, it reads as AI. Use judgment, not a mechanical filter.

## Before writing: the real fix

Most of these tells are symptoms of a deeper habit — writing in generalities instead of specifics. The single best fix is to **write toward a specific claim, example, or opinion** rather than a comprehensive-sounding overview. If you don't have a specific fact, anecdote, or stance for a sentence, that's usually a sign to cut the sentence rather than pad it with a stock phrase.

## Vocabulary and phrasing to avoid (or use sparingly)

Don't reach for these by default — they're fine once, but suspicious when they cluster:
- "Delve into," "at its core," "in the realm of," "it's important to note/understand," "crucial role in shaping," "today's fast-paced world/digital age," "sheds light on," "underscores," "plays a pivotal role," "stands as a testament to," "boasts," "a treasure trove of."
- Generic superlatives applied indiscriminately: "captivating," "majestic," "diverse and fascinating," "rich tapestry," "game-changing," "seamless."
- Vague hedge-attributions with no real source: "some critics argue," "many experts believe," "studies have shown" (without naming which).

## Structural and rhetorical patterns to avoid

- **Negative parallelism**: "It's not just X, it's Y." Fine once in a long piece; a tell when repeated.
- **Rule of three**: automatically grouping adjectives, benefits, or examples in threes ("innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking"). Vary the count — sometimes one strong word beats three vague ones, sometimes a real list has four or two items, not three by default.
- **Inflated-significance framing**: tying a minor or mundane detail to some larger historical/cultural importance — "marked a pivotal moment," "represented a significant shift," "part of a broader movement," "solidified its role," "highlights the enduring legacy." If the subject genuinely isn't that important, say so plainly instead of dressing it up.
- **Formulaic overview structure**: mechanically covering "Background → Challenges → Future Prospects" (or similar) regardless of whether the topic calls for it. Let the actual content dictate structure.
- **Over-transitioning**: leaning on "furthermore," "additionally," "moreover," "in conclusion," "overall," "in summary" to glue together ideas that don't need gluing. Often a sentence doesn't need a transition word at all — just start it.
- **Over-explaining**: spelling out basic or self-evident points the reader already understands, padding length without adding information.
- **Uniform, over-symmetric sentence rhythm**: sentences of similar length and shape in a row. Human writing varies — short and blunt, then longer and winding, on purpose.

## Formatting tics to avoid

- Excessive bold text used for emphasis rather than genuine structure.
- Title-casing headings that aren't actually titles.
- Bullet-pointing everything, including things better said as a sentence or two of prose.
- Stray em dashes doing the work of commas or periods throughout a piece — use one when it's the sharpest tool for that specific sentence, not as a reflex.
- Curly/smart-quote artifacts or emoji stuffed into headers/lists where they don't belong.

## What to do instead

- Ground claims in specifics: a real example, a number, a name, a lived detail — not a general statement about importance.
- Let some sentences be flat and short. Not everything needs a qualifier or a flourish.
- Take an actual position or observation where relevant, rather than surveying "on one hand / on the other."
- Vary sentence length and structure on purpose.
- Cut the concluding-paragraph reflex ("In summary...", "Overall...") — if the piece needs a close, end on the point that matters most instead of restating everything.

## Self-check before finalizing

Skim what you wrote and ask:
1. Did I use any phrase from the vocabulary list above more than once?
2. Is anything grouped in a suspiciously clean rule-of-three?
3. Did I use "not just X, it's Y" or a similar negative parallelism?
4. Does every paragraph end with an inflated-significance line or an "In summary"?
5. Are my sentences all roughly the same length and shape?
6. Could I replace a vague generality with one real, specific detail?

If several of these trip, rewrite that section rather than lightly editing it — the fix is usually structural, not word-swapping synonyms.
